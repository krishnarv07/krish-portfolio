Approach

I don't start with screens.
I start with questions.

I don’t use one fixed research method. I choose the right mix for the problem — from domain and competitor research to usability evaluation, testing, and measurable feedback.

What is this product, and who is it for?

Understand the context.

Before I draw anything, I learn how the product, people, business goal, and domain fit together — and how the experience works today.

  • Domain research — learn the vocabulary, rules, and constraints of the space.
  • Competitor review — see what users already expect and where others fall short.
  • User journeys — map each step people take and mark where they stall.

Journey audit: finding where friction hides (example)

Three questions I answer first

Who is this for?Their goals, habits, and level of experience.

What are they trying to finish?The real task behind the click.

What does the business need?The goal the design has to support.

Where does it break, and why?

Find where it breaks.

I evaluate the existing experience to uncover friction, unclear decisions, usability gaps, and edge cases — things a visual review can't show.

  • Heuristic review — score the interface against Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics.
  • Cognitive walkthrough — evaluate a task from a first-time user's perspective.
  • Task analysis — break goals into steps to spot extra effort.

Heuristic checklist

A fuller shape means healthier. Dents show where to dig deeper.

Cognitive walkthrough: order milk and a personal-care item with gift wrap

  • ✓Will the user try the right action?Shoppers expect to search for groceries.
  • ✕Will they notice the action is available?Gift wrap is hidden during product selection.
  • ✓Will they link the action to the result?"Add" is a pattern people already know.
  • ✓Will they see progress?The cart icon updates when an item is added.

How should it be organised?

Turn evidence into direction.

I turn research into patterns, priorities, and design direction — shaping clearer information, flows, and interactions before any visual polish.

  • Patterns and priorities — group findings and decide what matters most.
  • Information architecture — organise content the way people think.
  • Flows and wireframes — sketch the path, then the screens.

Hick's Law: RT = a + b·log₂(n + 1)

More choices at once means slower decisions, so I group, prioritise, and reveal options step by step. Constants are illustrative.

What this stage produces

Information architectureNavigation and content grouped by how users think.
User flowsEvery path and edge case, from entry to goal.
WireframesLow-fidelity layouts to test structure before style.

Did it actually get easier?

Test, learn, refine.

I validate prototypes with novice and expert users, using measurable signals. Then I iterate until the experience is clearer and ready to build.

  • Usability testing — task times and observation with real users.
  • UMUX — a two-question score for perceived usability.
  • NASA-TLX — weighted workload across mental, physical, and temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration.
